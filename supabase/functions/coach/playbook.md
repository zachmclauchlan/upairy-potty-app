# How parents feel, and "keep going or pause?"

## What parents are usually feeling
- **Failure and guilt:**
  - "I'm failing him."
  - Guilt about going back to nappies, about shouting once, about pushing a scared child.
- **Comparison:** "Everyone else's kid was done in 3 days." Social media and older siblings make normal week-2 accidents feel like failure.
- **Outside pressure:**
  - a health visitor's "she should be trained by now";
  - a nursery deadline to move up a room;
  - a school start date (especially summer-born children).
- **Exhaustion:** endless laundry, a newborn or pregnancy, ruined furniture.
- **Fear of harm:**
  - "Will I give her a phobia?"
  - "Will stopping make it harder later?"
  - "It's damaging our bond."
- **Embarrassment about asking the doctor**, especially about poo holding.

## How the coach responds (in this order)
1. **Validate in one line.** "Day 2 with no wees in the potty is really common, and really draining."
2. **Give a real, normal range.**
   - Most children take days to weeks to get the hang of wees, and weeks to months to be mostly accident-free.
   - Poos often lag wees by weeks or months.
   - The odd accident months later is normal.
   - Never promise "3 days".
3. **Two to four concrete steps for today or tomorrow.**
4. **Say clearly that pausing isn't failing,** when it fits: "A few months is nothing in the bigger picture."
5. **Reframe the setback as useful information:** "You're learning what motivates your child."
6. **For poo holding, nudge gently to the GP:** "It's common and medical, not naughty, and the GP wants to see it."

## Keep going or pause?
**Keep going (most cases)** when:
- the child is willing or neutral, even with lots of accidents;
- some wees are landing in the potty, or the child is noticing *during or after* (stopping mid-wee, saying "wet");
- it's day 1–5 or week 2. Many children "click" suddenly, often on day 4–5 or in week 2. Two days is too soon to judge.
- Week 2 is often *worse* than week 1, because the novelty has gone. That's normal, not failure.

**Pause properly** when:
- the child is distressed: crying, screaming at the potty, fighting every sit;
- they're holding wee for hours, or holding poo;
- nothing at all is clicking after about 2 weeks of consistent effort;
- the family is overwhelmed (new baby, illness, move).

**How to pause:**
- Go back to nappies or pull-ups without fuss. Present it as a choice, not a punishment: "We'll try big-kid pants again another day."
- Leave the potty out. No prompting. Read potty books at bedtime and let them see you use the toilet.
- Try again in **2–4 months**. Many children who struggled at about 2.5 trained in a day or two at around 2.75–3.
- Often the child announces it themselves: "I'm wearing pants."
- Going back to nappies for a while does **not** make it harder later. On-off training several days a week does confuse.

## What most often turns it around
- **Bottomless at home.** Pants can feel like a nappy. Use a long T-shirt with nothing underneath, and keep potties nearby.
- **Then commando:** loose trousers without underwear for a week or two. Add underwear once they're reliable.
- **Stop asking "Do you need a wee?"** The answer is always no. Announce instead, at transitions: "Potty time, then lunch." Use the same moments every day: on waking, before meals, before leaving the house, before the bath, before bed.
- **Commit fully.** No nappies in the daytime once you start, except nap and night. Don't do pants at nursery and pull-ups at home.
- **Don't start right before** a trip, a new baby or a nursery room change.
- **Never make them sit "until they go".** It builds fear.

## Words that help parents
- "This is normal. Most kids take weeks, not days, and accidents for a while afterwards are part of learning."
- "Pausing isn't giving up. It's choosing a better time."
- "Your child's timeline isn't the nursery's or the health visitor's."

# Readiness, methods, prompting, pants, rewards, words and timeline

## Readiness
- **Body:**
  - dry for 2+ hours, or wakes dry from a nap;
  - shows signs before going (grunting, freezing, squatting, going quiet, hiding);
  - can walk to the potty, sit for 2–5 minutes, and help with pants;
  - soft, regular poos.
- **Understanding:**
  - follows simple instructions;
  - tells you during or after (telling *before* means they're closer);
  - has words for wee and poo.
- **Feelings:**
  - dislikes wet or dirty nappies;
  - interested in the toilet (follows you in, wants to flush);
  - wants underwear or independence.
- **Norms:**
  - Most readiness skills appear between 22 and 30 months: girls earlier (about 24–26 months), boys about 29 months.
  - By 30 months the average child shows most signs.
- **Wait for signs or just start? The experts disagree:**
  - The AAP and child-led approaches say wait for signs.
  - The NHS, ERIC and Oh Crap say don't wait for the child to ask, because many never will (especially in very absorbent disposables).
  - **Coach line:** signs help, but a missing sign in a typical 2-year-old isn't a reason to wait forever.

## Age facts (use ranges)
- **Median age of daytime dryness:** girls about 32 months, boys about 35 months (US data). Huge cultural variation.
- **Best window for bladder and bowel health:** 18–30 months (ERIC). Oh Crap's ideal is 20–30 months.
- **Starting younger isn't harmful, but takes longer:**
  - Intensive training before 27 months took longer and didn't finish earlier.
  - It wasn't linked to constipation or poo refusal.
- **Starting after about 3–3.5:**
  - More poo refusal and more power struggles, but still very doable.
  - There's no "too late". The challenge just changes from learning to behaviour.
- **In a child-led group:** 61% were trained by 3, and 98% by 4.
- **Don't start during:** a move, a new baby, illness, a family crisis, or right before a trip or a nursery change.
- **Check poos first:** they should be soft, at least every other day. Treat constipation before starting.

## The methods: what parents actually do
No method is proven best. They have never been compared head to head. Fit the method to the child and the family.

### 3-day / intensive (Lora Jensen style)
- **Prep:**
  - Block out 3 days at home with one dedicated adult.
  - Cook ahead, and arrange care for siblings.
  - Get 20–30 pairs of pants or training pants, a potty, plenty of drinks, high-fibre snacks and small rewards.
  - Let the child pick their underwear the day before.
- **Day 1:**
  - Start when they wake. A "bye-bye nappies" moment, then T-shirt plus underwear, or bare bottom.
  - Lots of drinks. Keep saying "tell me when you need to wee."
  - Catch accidents mid-stream and calmly carry them to the potty to finish. This is the key skill.
  - Praise every success.
- **Days 2–3:** the same, with the child starting more trips themselves. Nappies for nap and night.
- **After day 3:** training isn't finished. Expect reminders and accidents for weeks, and poos usually take longer.
- **Prompting differs between sources** (every 15–20 min, versus no questions, just watch). In Potty Up, follow the app timer.

### Oh Crap! "blocks" (Jamie Glowacki)
- **Rules:**
  - 2–3 days at home, phone away, full attention on day 1.
  - Don't ask "do you need to go?" Prompt at their signals and at transitions.
  - No pull-ups in the day, and no deadline. Treats are optional (have an exit plan).
- **Block 1 – Naked from the waist down.**
  - Watch for signals (going still, tiptoeing, grabbing).
  - Catch the first dribble and get them to a potty kept close.
  - Move on when they're weeing and pooing in the potty with prompting, often 1–3 days.
- **Block 2 – Commando.**
  - Loose trousers, *no underwear* (underwear feels like a nappy).
  - Short active outings.
- **Block 3 – Outings.** Longer trips: shops, friends, daycare. Prompt before leaving, before activities and before bed.
- **Block 4 – Underwear.**
  - Back on at about 3 weeks.
  - A small accident spike is normal. If it's big, go back to commando.
- **Block 5 – Independence.**
  - They start trips on their own and accidents are rare.
  - Still prompt before leaving, before sleep, and when they're busy.
- **Nights** are handled later and separately.
- **Timeline:** the big hump usually passes in 7–10 days (about 2 weeks if under 20 months). Most finish the blocks in about 3 weeks.
- **If it goes wrong:** go back to the last block that worked.

### Bare bottom at home
Bottom half bare for long stretches at home, with plenty of drinks and the potty within reach. Don't ask them to sit; watch and catch. Build up to the commando stage and then pants (much like Oh Crap blocks 1–2).

### Gentle / child-led (Brazelton, AAP-style)
1. A child-size potty they pick. Clothed sits (while you use the toilet), with books and chat, for 1–2 weeks.
2. Nappy-off sits with no pressure. Tip nappy poos into the potty with them watching.
3. Sit them at natural times:
   - on waking;
   - 20–30 min after meals;
   - before and after sleeps;
   - before leaving the house;
   - before the bath and before bed;
   - at their usual poo time.
   That's about every 2 hours. Sits last 2–3 minutes, never forced.
4. Short nappy-free times with the potty nearby, then pants once they're doing well.
- **Timeline:** months, not days. Reminders for 1–2 months, and complete within about 6 months.

### Azrin & Foxx "less than a day" (historical)
- Uses a "wetting doll" demonstration, lots of drinks, sits, frequent dry-pants checks with praise, and a "hero is proud of you" line.
- Its original accident response (a telling-off plus 10 practice runs) goes against all modern guidance. Never recommend scolding.

### Elimination communication (from infancy)
Learning baby's timing and offering a potty with a cue sound. It's valid but not well studied, and builds communication more than continence. Keep it low-pressure.

## Prompting
- **Statements, not questions.** "Do you need to go?" gets a "no". Say instead:
  - "It's potty time."
  - "Potty, then lunch."
  - "Big potty or little potty?"
  - "Two minutes or five?"
  - "I'm going. Before me or after me?"
- **How often:**
  - **Intensive days 1–3:** every 15–30 min, or close watching while bare-bottomed.
  - **As they get more catches:** stretch to 1, then 1.5, then 2 hours.
  - **Long term:** mainly at transitions (waking, after meals and drinks, before leaving, sleep or bath, and during absorbed play).
  - Prompting too often can mean sits before the bladder is full, and they don't learn the feeling.
- Name their signs out loud: "You're dancing. That means a wee is coming. Let's go."
- Fade prompts once they're starting trips themselves: "Tell me when you get the potty feeling."
- **Drinks:**
  - Intensive methods load drinks on purpose, for more practice.
  - Otherwise just keep drinks and fibre up.
  - Don't quote exact daily millilitres for toddlers.

## Pants, pull-ups and nappies
- **Daytime:**
  - The strong consensus (AAP, NHS, NCT, ERIC, Oh Crap) is real underwear or **cloth/reusable training pants**, not disposable pull-ups.
  - Pull-ups are so absorbent that the child doesn't notice being wet, and they "feel like a nappy".
  - Cloth training pants let them feel wet while containing small accidents.
- **Sleep:** a nappy or pull-up is fine until they often wake dry. Do a potty sit before and after sleeps, and drop the nap nappy once naps are often dry.
- **Avoid switching back and forth** (pants at home, pull-ups at nursery). It confuses.
- **Honesty:** this is expert consensus plus small studies, not big trials. Don't overclaim. *(UpAiry makes reusable training pants. The coach can mention them only when the parent asks what to use.)*

## Rewards and praise
- **Experts disagree:**
  - NHS and most 3-day guides use stickers and small treats.
  - Oh Crap uses none.
  - The AAP says praise rather than treats on one page, and allows small rewards on another.
- **If using rewards:**
  - Make them small and immediate (a sticker, a high-five, a song).
  - Give them after the behaviour, not as a bribe beforehand.
  - Reward the *attempt* early on (sitting, telling you). The wee is a bonus.
  - Pair with specific praise, and fade out once it's consistent: every trip, then a good day, then praise only.
  - A separate small reward just for poo is common and optional.
- **Praise:**
  - Warm, not over the top. Big theatrics pressure anxious kids.
  - Shift ownership: "You must be really proud of yourself!" "Your belly must feel good!"
- **Pitfalls:**
  - escalating prizes;
  - a sugary treat for every wee;
  - taking rewards away;
  - "big boy/big girl" pressure.

## Words
- Use the same neutral words for wee, poo and body parts with every carer. Avoid "dirty", "naughty" or "stinky".
- **On the potty:**
  - "Let's try to do a wee in the potty."
  - If nothing comes: "Okay, we'll try again later."
  - A book or song helps.
- **Accidents:**
  - "Oops, you're still learning. Wee goes in the potty. Let's get changed."
  - "Thanks for telling me."
  - The child helps put wet clothes in the basket. Cooperative, never as punishment.
- **Success:**
  - "You did it! You must be so proud."
  - "Keep [the character on your undies] dry!"
  - Praise telling you, even if it's late.

## Timeline: what to tell parents
- **Days 1–3:** lots of accidents. Catches are often late or partial. Wees click before poos.
- **Days 4–10:** for most children, the hump passes. Poo held for nap or night nappies is common.
- **Weeks 2–4:**
  - Outings build up.
  - Accidents happen when busy, tired, excited or somewhere new.
  - Expect a small spike when underwear goes back on.
- **By about 4 weeks:** clear progress for most. If there's no interest or progress at all, it's okay to pause for a few weeks to 3 months. Make it a clean pause, not daily switching.
- **1–6 months:** reminders fade. Independence, wiping and public toilets mature. Most need wiping help until about 4–6.
- **"Trained":**
  - They notice the urge, hold it until the toilet, and manage pants.
  - Most wees and poos are in the toilet, and occasional accidents for months are normal.
  - Night dryness is separate.
- Many children are **mostly dry within 1–4 weeks** of focused training. Never promise 3 days.
- **See a doctor:**
  - not dry in the day by about 4 (evaluation by 5 at the latest);
  - accidents return after being trained;
  - ongoing constipation, painful poos or soiling;
  - frequent bedwetting at 7 (help available from 5).

# Poo holding, constipation, soiling, wees and wiping

**Safety rules:**
- The coach gives general information. It never diagnoses and never gives medicine doses.
- Before any laxative, suppository, enema or supplement, always say: "Please talk to your GP, health visitor or pharmacist before giving any medicine."
- If the parent mentions a red flag, put the red-flag advice first, before any tips.

## Why toddlers hold poo
1. **The pain cycle (most common).**
   - One hard or painful poo teaches "pooing hurts", so they hold on. The held poo gets bigger and harder, and the next one hurts more.
   - Small tears (fissures) cause bright red blood on the paper and make it worse.
   - It can be triggered by any change in poo: starting solids, a change of milk, more dairy, a tummy bug, antibiotics.
2. **Control.** Toddlers holding on as their own decision, often when training is pushed.
3. **Fear and anxiety:**
   - of the toilet, the flush, splash-back, or falling in;
   - big changes (a new baby, a move, starting nursery);
   - unfamiliar toilets;
   - being "too busy playing".

## Signs they're holding on
- Tiptoes, stiff straight legs, crossed legs, arching the back, squatting.
- A red face, hiding in a corner or behind the sofa.
- Saving poos for nap or night nappies, or asking for bed early.

## Signs of constipation
- Fewer than 3 poos a week, or 3+ days with none.
- Large or hard poos, "rabbit droppings", or poos so big they block the toilet.
- Straining, pain or crying, or blood on the paper.
- Tummy pain that comes and goes, or bloating.
- Poor appetite that improves after a big poo. Grumpy and tired.
- **Skid marks or smears** in their pants (often when passing wind).
- **Runny, very smelly leakage they don't feel.** This is overflow (see below).
- Constipation also causes more urine infections, daytime wetting and bedwetting.

## What a good poo looks like
- **Aim for:** a soft, smooth sausage (Bristol types 3–4, or 4–5 by some guides), easy to pass. Softness matters more than how often.
- **Normal frequency:** anywhere from 3 a day to every other day.
- **Constipated:** separate hard lumps, or a lumpy sausage (types 1–2).
- **Loose or watery** in a child known to be constipated may be overflow. Ask the doctor.
- **Coach rule of thumb:** aim for a soft poo every day or two. 3+ days without one, or any hard or painful poos → start the home plan and think about the GP.

## Overflow soiling ("encopresis")
- **What happens:** when hard poo stays put, the bowel stretches. The child stops feeling the urge, and runny poo leaks around the blockage without them knowing. Parents often think it's diarrhoea.
- **It is not deliberate or naughty.** The child can't control it.
- **Other signs:** hiding soiled pants, smell being the first clue, a sore bottom.
- **It needs a doctor.** Most children become accident-free with treatment, but it usually takes months.

## The order that works (most important)
1. **Get poos soft first, then work on behaviour.**
   - Hard or painful poos, or soiling → see the GP.
   - Most children with real constipation need a medicine plus home changes. Diet alone isn't enough treatment, and extra water alone doesn't fix constipation.
2. Then routine sits, good position, and small stepping stones.
3. Pressure is the top reason poo training backfires. Stay calm. Children pick up anxiety.

## Drinks and food
- **Drinks a day:** ages 1–3 about **900 ml**, ages 4–8 about **1.2 litres**. More in heat or when active. Mostly water.
  - Give them their own bottle. Pale wee means they're drinking enough.
- **Cow's milk:** no more than about **500 ml a day** over 18 months (too much can bind them up). No sweet drinks before meals. Avoid tea, coffee and cola.
- **Fibre:**
  - fruit with the skin on: plums, prunes, apricots, peaches, raisins;
  - **kiwifruit** daily;
  - vegetables, baked beans and peas, porridge, Weet-Bix, wholegrain bread and cereals;
  - prune juice mixed with apple juice, or frozen into ice blocks.
- **How much fibre:** about 5 servings of fruit and veg a day.
  - Don't add raw bran.
  - Always add water when adding fibre.
- **Daily active play helps too.**

## Routine sits after meals
- Eating makes the bowel move, so sit **15–30 min after breakfast and dinner** (and lunch if home), plus at their usual poo time.
  - Sit for 3–5 minutes with a sand timer. Never "until something comes".
  - Praise the sit, whether a poo comes or not.
- **Fearful child:**
  - Start with a 5-second sit, fully clothed, once a day, and reward it.
  - Don't mention poo during these sits. Build up slowly.
- When you see tiptoeing, hiding or wriggling, calmly take them to sit: "Poo wants to come out. Let's give it a comfy seat."
- Boys keep a sit routine even once they're weeing standing up.

## Position
- Feet flat on a step, never dangling, with knees higher than hips and apart.
- Lean forward with elbows on knees, back straight, and "push your tummy out".
- Use a child seat insert so they feel safe, or a potty that dips.

## Relaxing games (bottom relaxes, tummy pushes)
- **"Moo to poo":** a long "mooooo".
- **"Blow to go":** bubbles, party blowers, a harmonica, blowing a balloon, or blowing a ball through a straw. Keep these special for the toilet only.
- **"Rock to plop":** gently rock forward and back.
- A warm evening bath helps them relax. Breathe into the tummy, and don't strain. Never "push harder!"

## "Will only poo in a nappy": stepping stones
**Key rule: if they ask for a nappy to poo, don't say no.** Refusing leads to holding on and constipation. Move up one step only when they're relaxed. If they get upset, go back a step. This takes weeks or months.

1. Treat any constipation first (with the GP).
2. **Make the toilet less scary:**
   - Flush while they stand at the door, and come closer each time. Let them flush a bit of paper.
   - Food colouring in the cistern, and guess the colour.
   - Toilet paper on the water stops splash fear. Ear defenders help with noise.
3. Happy clothed sits that have nothing to do with poo.
4. **The nappy only in the bathroom:**
   - Change every poo nappy next to the toilet.
   - They tip the poo into the toilet, flush and wash hands.
   - If they always poo in one spot (behind the sofa), move that spot towards the bathroom a little every few days.
5. Squatting in the nappy beside the toilet.
6. **Sitting on the toilet wearing the nappy** for poos (with the step and seat insert).
7. Loosen the nappy: undo one tab, then all of them, then lay it open on the seat. Or cut a hole in it so the poo drops through, and make the hole bigger over time.
8. The nappy (or a piece of it) laid in the bowl, getting smaller.
9. A pad of toilet paper, then nothing.

## Books, stories, rewards
- Books help: "Poo Goes to Pooland" is a favourite with parents, and there are continence charity stories.
  - Use books and toys early to make sitting pleasant.
  - Once they sit happily, switch to active "moo and blow" sits.
- **Rewards for effort** (sitting, drinking, following the plan), not for results.
  - A small treat just for poo is common.
  - Keep it low-key for anxious children. Rewards alone don't fix fear-driven holding.
- No repeated "Do you need a poo?" Use statements: "It's time to sit after breakfast."
- Privacy helps: close the door for a minute, and stay nearby if they want you.

## Words
**To the child:**
- "You can have your nappy for your poo. Let's put it on in the bathroom."
- "Feet on your step, elbows on knees. Let's be a cow: moooo."
- "You sat for the whole timer. That's brilliant sitting."
- "Poos sometimes surprise us. Let's get clean pants on together."
- "Your medicine makes poo soft and slippery so it won't hurt."
- "Lots of kids find pooing tricky. We'll work it out together."
- To an older child with soiling: "Your tummy got stretched and stopped sending the poo message. It's not your fault, and the plan will help."

**To the parent:**
- "The poo is the problem, not your child."
- "Soiling isn't naughty."
- "Soft poos first, potty skills after."

**Never:**
- "No more nappies, you have to use the potty."
- Anger, punishment, disgust faces, "You're too big for this."
- "Push harder."
- Making them sit until something comes.
- Stopping prescribed medicine on your own once poos look good.

## Red flags
**Same day (GP urgently, NHS 111 or healthline; 111/999/A&E if very unwell):**
- a swollen, hard tummy, especially with vomiting, or severe tummy pain;
- very unwell, floppy, drowsy or confused;
- fever with pain when weeing;
- not pooing at all and in pain, or refusing food because of discomfort;
- a baby under 12 months who seems constipated.

**Tell the doctor straight away** (these need a specialist, not just constipation treatment):
- constipation since birth or the first weeks of life;
- weakness in the legs, or delayed walking;
- a hairy patch, deep dimple or odd marks on the lower back;
- thin ribbon-like poos;
- a family history of Hirschsprung's disease;
- poor growth or weight loss.

**GP soon (not an emergency):**
- any toddler who seems constipated (getting help early makes it easier);
- blood on the paper;
- soiling, skid marks or leakage in a trained child;
- holding on, or real distress about pooing;
- no improvement after 1–2 weeks of home changes;
- still in pain or soiling while already on treatment;
- constipation lasting more than 8 weeks.

## What doctors usually do (explain in general terms only, never doses)
- They examine the child and check for red flags. X-rays and blood tests usually aren't needed for ordinary constipation.
- **If the bowel is blocked:** a "clear-out" with a macrogol (polyethylene glycol) medicine mixed into a cold drink, at a dose the doctor sets.
  - Common brands: Movicol Paediatric, Laxido or CosmoCol (UK and NZ), MiraLAX (US).
  - Other options a doctor may add: senna, lactulose.
  - **Warn parents:** the clear-out can make soiling and tummy pain worse at first. A doctor usually reviews it within about a week.
- **Then a daily maintenance dose** adjusted to keep poos soft:
  - It's continued for weeks after a regular habit forms (often months).
  - It's kept going while toilet training is underway.
  - It's stopped by tapering slowly, never suddenly.
- **These medicines are safe for toddlers, and children don't become dependent on them.** Stopping too early is the common mistake. Relapse means they still need help.
- Enemas and suppositories only on a doctor's instruction.
- **The coach never gives doses** (no "a capful per year of age"), and never tells a parent to start, stop or change a medicine.

## Wee problems
**Holding wee** (crossing legs, wriggling, waiting until bursting, leaking just before the toilet):
- Holding for a long time raises the risk of a urine infection.
- Offer a wee about every 2–3 hours: on waking, before going out, after meals, before bed, and whenever they feel the need.
- Sit relaxed with feet supported and knees apart, and don't rush.

**Frequent wees and urgency:**
- Can be an overactive bladder.
- **Constipation is a big hidden cause**, because a full bowel presses on the bladder. Treating poos often fixes wee problems.

**Urine infection signs:**
- fever, being sick, off food, irritable, tummy pain;
- stinging when weeing, going often or urgently;
- new wetting in a dry child;
- blood in the wee, or cloudy wee.
→ **See a doctor urgently that day.** A urine test confirms it. Dark or smelly wee alone may just mean they need more drinks.

**Preventing urine infections:**
- treat constipation, and don't hold on;
- a footstool so they empty fully;
- girls wipe front to back;
- cotton underwear, and no scented soaps or bubble baths.

**Daytime wetting after being trained:**
- **Causes:** putting it off while playing, an overactive bladder, not emptying fully, constipation, a urine infection, life changes. Never the child's fault.
- **See a doctor** if it keeps happening. Keep a few days' diary first (wee times, accidents, drinks, poos).
- **Constant dribbling, always-damp pants, a weak stream or straining** always needs a health professional.

## Wiping and hygiene
- **Toddlers (about 2–3):**
  - You wipe and narrate: **front to back**, leaning forward or standing.
  - They help with the easy parts: tipping poo into the toilet, flushing, washing hands.
- **Learning to wipe (about 3–4+):**
  - The adult does the first wipes and the child does the last one, then the last two, and so on.
  - About 3 sheets at a time: "One square, two squares, three squares, tear!"
  - Practise away from the toilet (reaching behind, passing a ball back to back).
  - Many children need checking and finishing until 4–6.
- **Skid marks once they're wiping:** still learning, but persistent smears can be an early constipation sign. Check their poos.
- **Wipes:** use toilet paper, and **bin wipes even if they say "flushable".** Never flush nappy pieces or pull-ups.
- **Hand washing every time:** soap for about 20 seconds (sing "Happy Birthday" twice), rinse, and dry fully.

# Nights, naps and bedwetting

## Why nights come later
- Night dryness depends on three things maturing on their own timetable, separate from daytime skills:
  1. **Night hormone (vasopressin/ADH)** that makes the kidneys produce less, more concentrated wee overnight. Low levels: wetting in the first 2–3 hours of sleep, large wet patch, pale wee.
  2. **Bladder capacity and stability.** Small or "overactive" bladder: wets more than once a night, small patches, may wake after wetting.
  3. **Waking to the full-bladder signal.** Not "too deep a sleeper": the brain doesn't yet notice the signal in sleep. Sleeps through wetting, often later in the night.
- It runs strongly in families. Boys are about twice as likely as girls until about 10. Constipation makes it worse (a full bowel presses on the bladder).
- It is never laziness or poor training, and trying harder doesn't speed it up.

## What's normal (quote ranges, not exact numbers)
- Most children become dry at night between 3 and 5. Daytime dryness usually comes months or years earlier.
- About 1 in 7 five-year-olds still wet the bed sometimes, and about 1 in 10 at 6–7. About 5% at 10.
- It resolves on its own for roughly 1 in 10 affected children each year.
- Under 5: normal, no treatment needed. 5+: help is available if it bothers the child or family. Many doctors don't treat before 6–7 unless there are other symptoms.

## When to try nights without a nappy
Any of these:
- dry or nearly dry nappies most mornings for 1–2 weeks;
- reliably dry in the day for 6+ months;
- the child asks to stop night nappies.

How to run the trial:
- Choose a calm 1–2 weeks: no travel, new baby, house move or illness.
- Put protection on the bed, not the child, so they can feel wet.
- Judge after 1–2 weeks, not one night.
- **Mostly dry or improving:** keep going.
- **Soaked almost every night with no change:** go back to night pants without fuss and retry in 1–3 months. Say "not yet", never "you failed".
- **5+ and still wet:** a nappy-free trial of 1–2 weeks is still worthwhile, because feeling wet can be the trigger. If it's still wet after that, see the GP.

## Naps
- Naps are usually dry before nights, so they're good practice.
- Offer a potty sit right before the nap. Keep big drinks to earlier, and put a waterproof layer under the child.
- If naps are often dry, drop the nap nappy.

## Fluids
- **Don't cut drinks across the day.** It doesn't help and can backfire. A thirsty child drinks more at night, and concentrated wee irritates the bladder.
- Offer drinks evenly: one at each meal and snack, mostly water (milk is fine). Ages 4–8 need roughly 1–1.4 litres a day. Toddlers: regular drinks through the day.
- Front-load the day. Most drinks should be before late afternoon.
- **Last drink about 1 hour before bed**, then nothing until morning (a small sip is fine if they're really thirsty). Some sources say 2 hours; 1 hour is enough for a well-hydrated child.
- No caffeine: cola, iced tea, hot chocolate, energy drinks. Avoid fizzy and sugary drinks after lunch.
- Check poos: fewer than 3 a week, hard or large poos, straining or soiling means treat constipation first. Night wetting often improves once poos are soft.

## Bedtime routine
1. A normal drink with dinner, then stop about 1 hour before bed.
2. Wind down with a book and low lights, and no screens in the last hour.
3. **Bedtime wee** as part of getting ready: relaxed, no rushing. Do a "last wee" right before lights out if more than 30 minutes have passed.
4. Make night trips easy: a night light, a potty in the bedroom, pyjamas that are easy to pull down.
5. Tell them: "If you feel a wee coming in the night, the potty is right there, and you can call me any time."
6. In the morning, wee straight after waking, and praise the routine, not the result.

## Lifting or dream-wees (carrying a sleeping child to the toilet)
- NHS, NICE and children's hospitals: don't rely on it. It may save a sheet, but it doesn't teach dryness, and can teach weeing while asleep.
- It's okay as a short-term fix in a tough week (a holiday, a new baby). Be honest that it isn't training.
- Do help if the child wakes up on their own.

## Bed setup: "double-make" the bed
- From the bottom up: waterproof mattress protector, absorbent pad or bed mat, sheet, then a second pad or mat and a second sheet on top.
- After a wet, strip only the top layer. No remaking the bed at 2am.
- For very wet children, add waterproof duvet and pillow protectors.

## Handling a wet night
1. Low light, quiet voice, brief and matter-of-fact. "Uh-oh, a wee came out. Let's get you dry and cosy."
2. Wee in the toilet, change pyjamas, strip the top layer, back to bed.
3. In the morning they can help put pyjamas in the wash basket. That's responsibility, never punishment.
4. Don't discuss it at breakfast or in front of siblings, and allow no teasing.

## Rewards at night
- **Reward what the child can control, not dry nights:**
  - drinking their water during the day;
  - doing the bedtime wee;
  - getting up to the potty;
  - helping change the bed.
- Never take away stickers they've already earned. Rewarding dry nights rewards something their body can't do yet, and adds shame.

## Bedwetting alarms (age 5+, usually most successful from 6–7)
- A moisture sensor sounds or vibrates at the first drops. Over weeks, the brain learns "full bladder means wake up".
- They help about half to three-quarters of children, and are the option most likely to give lasting dryness. Don't promise a result.
- **How to use one:**
  - Use it every night. The child helps set it up and switches it on.
  - Put the unit far enough away that they must get out of bed to turn it off.
  - **Expect to help at first.** Many children don't wake to it in the early weeks, so a parent wakes them fully and walks them through it: turn it off, finish on the toilet, change, reset.
  - Signs it's working: smaller patches, waking faster, wetting later in the night.
  - Review at about 4 weeks. Continue until 14 dry nights in a row, which usually takes 6–8 weeks.
  - If it's not improving after 4 weeks, talk to the GP. If wetting returns, using the alarm again often works.
- Make the child the hero: "This alarm is like a coach for your brain."

## Medicines
- Desmopressin is a medicine some doctors prescribe from about 6. It reduces night-time wee on the nights it's taken (useful for sleepovers and camps). It isn't a cure.
- With desmopressin, fluids must be limited from 1 hour before to 8 hours after the dose, and it's paused during vomiting or diarrhoea.
- **The coach never suggests doses or starting medicine. Always say: talk to your GP.**

## See a doctor
**Book a GP visit (not urgent):**
- 5+ and still wetting despite a good routine, or it's upsetting the child;
- wetting starts again after 6+ months dry (also think about stress: a new sibling, school, bullying);
- daytime symptoms: urgency, daytime wetting, weeing more than 7 or fewer than 4 times a day, straining or a weak stream;
- constipation or poo accidents;
- loud snoring or pauses in breathing at night;
- repeated urine infections;
- no improvement after 4 weeks with an alarm.

**See a doctor the same day:**
- pain or burning when weeing, fever, or the child seems unwell (possible urine infection);
- blood or pink or cloudy wee;
- very thirsty, weeing large amounts, tired, losing weight (possible diabetes).

## Words that help
- To the child:
  - "Lots of kids' bodies are still learning to stay dry at night. It's not your fault, and you're not in trouble."
  - "Your brain and bladder are still learning to talk to each other while you sleep."
- To the parent:
  - "Two weeks is a fair test. Going back to night pants isn't failure. It just means not yet."
  - "Night dryness depends on a hormone that matures on its own timetable."

## Never
- Punish, shame or call it lazy. Remove earned rewards. Let siblings tease. Compare with other children.
- Restrict drinks all day. Threaten nappies. Buy "cure" products with big claims.

_Sources: NHS bedwetting; NICE CG111 (via patient.info clinical summary); ERIC night-time wetting factsheet; AAP HealthyChildren bedwetting; Kids Health NZ; Royal Children's Hospital Melbourne; Cochrane review of alarm interventions; MyHealth Alberta._

# Tricky situations

**Rules that apply to everything below:**
- Rule out constipation and urine infection early.
- Never punish, shame or force a sit.
- Get every carer using the same words and approach.
- Only about 2–3% of children have real toilet-training *problems*. Most bumps are normal.

## 1. Refusal, "NO!" to every prompt, tantrums at the potty
**Why it happens:**
- Toddlers are discovering autonomy, and constant asking or hovering invites "no".
- Sometimes the child is simply not ready.
- Fear or pain (constipation, the flush, falling in). A "slow-to-warm" child's defiance is often anxiety.
- Two kinds of resistance:
  - **Uncertainty** (unsure of the change): respond with calm confidence and less hovering.
  - **Defiance** (can do it, but would rather play): respond with small, immediate, logical consequences, like helping to clean up.

**Plan:**
1. Check poos: hard, infrequent or painful → treat that first.
2. **Stop asking yes/no questions.** Use statements at transitions, and prompt once, then step back. "Potty, then lunch."
3. Keep sits short (2–3 minutes) and pleasant (a book or song). They can get up when they want.
4. Give two choices that both lead to the potty: "Frog potty or big toilet?" "Wipes or paper?"
5. Name the feeling, then hold the line calmly: "You're cross it's potty time. I get it. Potty, then back to the blocks."
6. Mid-tantrum: don't drag or hold them on it. "Your body, your job. The potty's right here when you're ready." Then walk away calmly.
7. Treat accidents neutrally, and have the child help clean up.
8. Avoid "big boy/big girl" labels. They can shame when the child falls short.

**Pause if:**
- they cry or fight every sit for several days;
- they start holding wee or poo;
- the parent is getting angry.

**How to pause:**
- Say calmly that nappies are back for a while, put the potty away, and restart in 2–4 weeks (up to 1–2 months).
- **Restart only once.** For over-3s, prefer adjustments over a full reset: pants plus a potty nearby, more transition prompts.

**Poo-only refusal** (wees on the potty, won't poo there, about 1 in 4 children): see the poo section. Treat constipation, use positive poo words, and allow a nappy in the bathroom as a bridge.

## 2. Regression (new sibling, move, daycare, illness, holiday)
- Common triggers: a childcare change, a new baby or pregnancy, a move, illness, a death, parental conflict. Also constipation or a urine infection.
- **One accident isn't regression.** A pattern is several a day, or rising, or only at daycare. "True" regression is about 6+ months after daytime nappies ended. Before that, they may not have been fully trained.
- **It usually lasts days to a few weeks.** More than a month → reassess readiness and move forward gradually.

**Plan:**
1. Look for the trigger. If there's none, see the GP to check constipation or a urine infection.
2. Name it gently: "I've noticed more accidents lately. I wonder if it's because of the new baby. Do you want to tell me about it?"
3. Address the trigger:
   - **New baby:** daily one-to-one "big kid time" that has nothing to do with the potty. Involve them in the baby's care, and don't compare them with the baby.
   - **Move:** set up the potty and step in the new bathroom on day 1, and walk the route.
   - **Daycare:** visit the bathroom together and bring familiar things.
4. Back to basics: regular reminders ("busy kids forget"), and bring back the sticker chart or small rewards that worked before.
5. Stay calm. Children pick up anxiety.
6. Offer compromises rather than a full reset: a potty in their room, or walk them to the daycare bathroom before you leave.

**Get help:**
- **See a doctor:** fever, pain when weeing, pink wee, fewer than 2 poos a week.
- **Contact a professional immediately** if there's any sign of physical, psychological or sexual harm. Regression can occasionally be a safeguarding signal.
- **Illness:** accidents with diarrhoea or tiredness are expected. Resume normally once they're well.

## 3. Daycare and preschool
- Policies vary. Some preschools want children trained by 3. Many centres count "trained" as using the toilet most of the time with about 1–2 accidents a day. UK guidance: settings shouldn't wait for children with additional needs.

**Ask the centre:**
- Underwear or pull-ups? Group toilet times or individual?
- Do staff remind, or wait to be asked? Is there a step stool and child-size toilet?
- How are accidents handled? Pull-ups at nap? Stickers?
- How will they report back?

**Align home and centre:**
- Use the same words for wee and poo, and match their schedule at home (after meals, before nap).
- Start training **well before** the first day, not during the transition.

**Daily bag:**
- 3–4 full changes (undies, pants, socks, shoes if possible).
- A wet bag, wipes, and clothes they can manage alone (elastic waists, no snaps or overalls).
- Label everything.

**Handover note the coach can draft:**
> "[Name] is on day [X], in underwear during the day. Their sign is [jiggling/going quiet]. They usually need to go about [45–90] min after drinks or meals. Our words are 'wee' and 'poo'. Please tell, don't ask: 'Time to try before outside play.' For accidents, a calm 'Wees go in the potty, let's get changed' is perfect. Last poo: [time]. Could you tell me at pick-up how many tries, wees and accidents there were? Thank you!"

**Problems:**
- **Accidents at daycare but fine at home:** this is normal for weeks to months (busy, exciting, other kids in nappies, unfamiliar or shared toilets, no privacy). Ask staff to offer on a schedule (every 1.5–2 h, or before snack and outdoor play) and allow privacy.
- **Fine at daycare, bad at home:** copy daycare's routine at home.
- **Daycare insists on pull-ups:**
  - Ask whether they'll accept cloth or reusable training underwear, which holds a small accident but still feels wet.
  - If not, use pull-ups there only ("daycare pants"), with underwear at home and on weekends.
  - Switching back and forth can confuse some kids, so keep it consistent.
- **Starting school with accidents:** talk to the school early. They have intimate care plans and have seen it before. UK schools shouldn't refuse a child because of continence difficulties.

## 4. Outings and travel
- **The golden rule: a try before leaving, before the car, before an activity.** "Potty first, then shoes."
- Build up: start with an hour at the park, then errands, then cafés and soft play over about a week.
- **Car:** stop every 1–2 hours, keep a travel potty in the boot, and put a car seat pad or puppy pad under them with a spare. Offer drinks earlier rather than in the car, but don't restrict.
- **Planes:** a try just before boarding, and use the plane toilet early (before the seatbelt sign or queues). Warn them about the loud flush and let them leave the cubicle first, or cover their ears. Pack a folding seat insert.
- **Portable potties and folding seats:** practise with them at home first.
- **Public toilet fears:**
  - **Auto-flush:** cover the sensor with a sticky note (let the child stick it on). Call them "robot toilets": "Let's give it its sticker so it stays asleep."
  - **Hand dryers:** warn first, then ear covers or ear defenders, or use a wipe instead. Practise at home with a hairdryer the child controls.
  - **Big seats:** sit them well back or slightly sideways while you hold them, or use a seat cover or insert.
- **Tell café staff you're potty training.** They'll often let you use the staff toilet.
- **Pull-ups for travel:** optional as a backup on long trips only ("travel pants"), worn over underwear if possible so they still feel wet. Back to underwear the moment you arrive.
- Don't *start* training right before a big trip. Sugary drinks mean very frequent wees.

## 5. Boys and girls
**Boys:**
- **Sit for everything first** (it catches poos, and there's less mess). Teach standing once he reliably notices the urge and gets there in time, often around 3–3.5.
- **Sitting:** lean forward and tuck the penis down. Sitting backwards (facing the cistern) helps aim and balance.
- **Standing:** watch Dad if the family is comfortable with it, stand close, point down, and use a target ("sink the Cheerio", a ping-pong ball, a target sticker).
- **Keep a daily sit for poos.** Some boys forget poos once they're standing, and constipation follows.
- Potty splash guards can hurt. Choose a low or soft guard, and teach him to tuck down.

**Girls:**
- Wipe **front to back** ("like a slide going down"), and pat dry after wees. Most children can only wipe properly alone at about 4, so check and finish until then.
- **Preventing urine infections:**
  - Don't let her hold wee for long: offer every 1–2 h early on.
  - Avoid bubble baths and soapy bath water.
  - Keep drinks up and poos soft.
  - Use a footstool so she empties fully.
- **Signs of a urine infection:** stinging, urgency, smelly or pink wee, fever, new accidents. See a doctor.

## 6. Fear of the potty, toilet, flushing or falling in
- **Causes:** imagination, not knowing what will happen, noise, fear of falling, a past painful poo, or feeling a "part of them" is going away.

**Plan:**
1. Rule out painful poos.
2. Let them choose: a floor potty (less scary) or the toilet with a **seat insert and a step stool** (feet flat, so they feel secure).
3. **Many "the potty is scary" kids do better going straight to the toilet** with an insert. The potty can feel babyish.
4. Tiny steps:
   - Sit clothed for 30 seconds, then build up.
   - Read toilet books, and watch family use it ("I use it too, it's safe").
5. **Flush desensitising:**
   - Flush at the very end, after hands are dry, or after they've left the room.
   - Then they stand in the doorway, then come closer each day, until they press it themselves.
   - Use ear defenders, and let them flush toilet paper to "wave bye-bye".
6. Tiny reward just for sitting.
7. Wee in the bath or outside on grass helps a child who can't let go.
8. Never force a sit. If fear leads to holding or panic, step back to the floor potty, or a nappy in the bathroom for poos. If it persists, talk to the GP or health visitor.

**Phrases:**
- "Your bottom is too big to fit down there. The seat holds you tight."
- "You're the boss of the flush. Now, or after we wash hands?"

## 7. Only goes for one parent, or won't go at Grandma's
- **Causes:** trust in one person's routine, different words or approach from another carer, an embarrassing accident there, or an unfamiliar toilet.

**Plan:**
1. Write a one-page "how we do it" sheet for every carer: the same words, timing, prompts, praise level and accident response.
2. At home, the "preferred" parent steps back for a few days while the other parent handles bathroom routines.
3. The other carer models it.
4. Take the familiar kit to Grandma's: their seat, step, book or toy. Tour the bathroom on arrival with a no-pressure sit.
5. Ask grandparents: no "do you need to go?", no fuss over accidents, no "big boy" comments. "Just say 'Potty time, then snack.'"
6. If one place causes distress, a temporary backup there is fine (their own potty, or training pants). Keep home steady.

## 8. "Too busy playing", accidents weeks or months in, "I don't need to go"... then wet
- **Why:**
  - Absorbed play blocks the signal.
  - Many 2-year-olds genuinely can't sense a full bladder until it's urgent (about 3 in 10 two-year-olds have good bladder sensation, versus 8 in 10 three-year-olds).
  - "No" is often honest: they don't feel it *yet*.
  - Constipation causes urgency and leaks.
- **What counts as trained:** fewer than about 4 wee accidents a week and no more than about 2 poo accidents a month. An occasional play accident is not regression.

**Plan:**
1. Stop asking. Use transition prompts: before going outside, before screens, before snack, before the car.
2. "Sit and try" every 1–2 h early on, and sooner after big drinks.
3. Make stopping easy:
   - Put the potty near the play area.
   - "Play pauses for potty. Your tower will wait right here."
   - Let them bring a small toy.
   - Use a timer, or a vibrating watch for older kids.
4. **"I don't need to go":** "Okay, you don't have to wee. Just sit while we sing one song / count to 10."
5. Keep a 3-day log of drinks, wees and accidents to find their natural interval. Prompt just before it. (Potty Up's timer does this automatically from their logs.)
6. Check poos. Small frequent wees, skid marks or poo accidents weeks in may be constipation.
7. After an accident: "Oops, wees go in the potty. Let's get dry clothes. You can put the wet ones in the bag." Then back to play. Never "You said you didn't need to go!".
8. **See a doctor:**
   - accidents increasing after months dry;
   - daytime wetting after about 4;
   - repeated urine infections;
   - constipation.

## 9. Twins, older children, and additional needs
**Twins and multiples:**
- Go by each child's readiness, not their age. Together or separately both work.
- One potty per child, kept within reach. Short sits at set times, statements not questions.
- Expect more accidents while you're watching several, and loose simple clothes help.
- Avoid comparison: "Everyone's body learns at its own speed."

**Older children (3.5–4+):**
- **Rule out constipation and poo holding first.** They're more common in late trainers.
- Give them ownership:
  - they choose their underwear and set their own timer or watch;
  - they help with laundry as responsibility, not punishment.
- Avoid babyish framing: "You're in charge of your body. My job is to help. What would help you remember?"
- Prefer adjustments over nappies-back resets for over-3s.
- **See a doctor:**
  - frequent accidents past 4;
  - no progress after months;
  - repeated urine infections or constipation;
  - fear.
- About 98% of children are dry in the day by 4. By about 5, most can go to the toilet alone.

**Autism, developmental delay, sensory differences:**
- **Don't wait for readiness signs.** Autism organisations suggest preparing early (even before 2), because a preference for sameness makes later change harder. Children with additional needs can learn; they may need more time.
- **Constipation is common** (restricted diets, low drinks, avoiding poos). Check it first.
- **Visual schedule** next to the toilet: pants down → sit → wee/poo → wipe → pants up → flush → wash → dry. Use exact pictures, and cover each step as it's done. Read a social story before practice.
- **Routine:**
  - Change nappies in the bathroom, and keep the same sequence every time.
  - Change one thing at a time.
  - Everyone starts on the same day, with a home–school book.
- **Timed toileting:**
  1. Observe the pattern for a few days, then take them at those times.
  2. Use a quiet toilet-only toy, and count to 10 or use a sand timer before getting up.
  3. Use vibrating watch prompts for older kids.
  4. A now/next board: "toilet, then iPad".
- **Sensory:**
  - Check strong soap smells, a noisy fan, bright lights and water temperature, and keep the room calm.
  - Add foot supports, side rails, or a smaller seat. Physical security matters.
- **Comfort in a full nappy:** give that comfort another way (a heavy, warm blanket). For poos, the child wears the nappy while sitting on the toilet, and you cut it away gradually.
- **Washable training pants** help them feel wet.
- **Rewards:** match them to the child. Social praise can be hard to process, and a preferred activity after the routine may work better.
- Many train at 5–7, so follow their pace. If it becomes distressing, take a break of about 3 months.
- **Get continence support:** a paediatrician, a continence nurse, ERIC or Bladder & Bowel UK.
- Don't assume every problem is "the autism": other continence issues may need treatment.

## 10. Clothes and winter
- They must be able to get clothes down fast:
  - elastic-waist joggers or leggings;
  - warm socks instead of tights.
- **Avoid:**
  - onesies with snaps, overalls or dungarees;
  - belts, buttons and tights;
  - footed pyjamas and all-in-one snowsuits.
- **At home:**
  - bottomless → commando (trousers, no underwear) → underwear;
  - in winter, heat the main room rather than adding layers, and add a long jumper.
- **Warm bathroom:**
  - a rug at the potty;
  - a seat insert so they're not on a cold seat;
  - a second potty in the warmest room.
- **Out:**
  - a toilet stop before coats and snowsuits go on;
  - a full spare outfit including socks.
- **Chant:** "Coat off, pants down, sit!"

# What real parents say worked (and what backfired)

## Poo
- Treat constipation first. GP-guided stool softeners (Movicol, Laxido, lactulose) are often kept up for months, then weaned. Once stools are soft, the behaviour often follows.
- Fade the nappy:
  1. Nappy only in the bathroom.
  2. Poo in the nappy while sitting on the toilet.
  3. Cut a small hole, and make it bigger over weeks.
  4. Only the waistband left, then nothing.
  - Variation: a nappy laid in the bowl under the seat.
- Blowing bubbles or a toy only on the toilet (relaxes the pelvic floor).
- Feet supported and a secure seat (a family seat with a child ring, plus a sturdy step).
- Sit after meals at the child's usual poo time. Not rushed. Distraction helps some children, not all.
- Books: "Poo Goes to Pooland" (book, app and video) was often credited. Also NHS and ERIC stories.
- A small treat only for poo (or a bigger one for poo than for wee). Keep it low-key for anxious kids.
- If they hide to poo, move the potty to the hiding spot and edge it closer to the bathroom each day.
- A warm evening bath helps them relax.
- Backfired: forcing sits while crying, big reward escalations, treating it as defiance.

## Refusing to sit, fear, holding wee
- Skip the potty and go straight to the toilet with a seat insert and a step. This is the most common fix for "the potty is scary/babyish".
- Model it: narrate your own trips, and let them flush and wave bye-bye.
- A tiny reward just for sitting, so the potty stops being scary.
- Wee in the bath, a tepid shower, or outside on the grass helps a child who can't let go.
- Holding wee for hours or sobbing → stop and wait a few weeks (risk of fear, UTIs, constipation).

## Not noticing the urge
- Real underwear or thick cloth training pants rather than pull-ups, so they feel wet.
- Prompt about every hour or at transitions, not every 15–20 min. Stretch the interval over time.
- Log wee times and offer about 30 min before their usual times.
- Name their tells: going quiet, stopping play, dancing, holding themselves.
- Accidents are an inconvenience, not a punishment: a calm stock phrase, then they help strip off and put clothes in the wash. Use easy clothes a size up.
- Uses the potty when naked but forgets when dressed → go commando in loose trousers for 1–2 weeks.
- A wee game: wee into a measuring cup and read the number.

## Daycare and school
- Accidents at nursery are normal for weeks or months after home is reliable (busy, exciting, other kids in nappies, unfamiliar toilets).
- Agree a schedule with staff (every 1.5–2 h, or before snack and outdoor play) instead of "do you need to go?".
- Send the chart to nursery and give prizes at home. Pack lots of spare clothes and bags.
- Talk to the school early: they have intimate care plans and have seen it before.
- Good at nursery but bad at home → copy nursery's routine at home.
- Daycare rules like "two weeks accident-free before underwear" slow progress. Ask if they'll accept cloth training pants.

## Regression
- Stay calm and low-fuss. It often settles in about 2 weeks.
- One-to-one time with the older child, and involve them in the baby's care.
- A brief return to pull-ups after a new baby is okay. Most recover within weeks.
- A daily "dry pants" sticker for dribbling.
- Rule out constipation or a urine infection first (AAP).

## Outings
- A wee before leaving the house is part of going out.
- Start with an hour at the local park, then build up to cafés and soft play over about a week.
- Pack a travel potty, spare clothes, wipes, bags, and a puppy pad on the car seat.
- Pull-ups only for flights and long drives, worn over undies so they still feel wet. No setback reported.
- Tell café staff you're potty training. They'll often let you use the staff toilet.
- Auto-flush fear: a sticky note over the sensor. Hand-dryer fear: cover their ears or use ear defenders, and practise at home with a hairdryer they control.
- Sugary drinks mean very frequent wees.

## Boys
- Sit for everything first (it catches poos too). Stand later, at about 3–3.5, once fully trained.
- Tuck the penis down and sit them well back to stop spray.
- Sitting backwards on the toilet (facing the cistern) helps aim and balance. Nurseries use it.
- A boy who won't sit: let him stand, with a target in the bowl.

## Autism, ADHD, additional needs
- A now/next picture board ("toilet, then iPad"), social stories, and straight to the toilet rather than a potty.
- Physical security: a seat with handles and a sturdy step (an OT tip).
- Fade nappies gradually. A strongly wanted toy for the first 10 successes.
- An intimate care plan at school. Many children train at 5–7. Follow the child's pace.

# Myths the coach should gently correct

- **"If it doesn't work in 3 days, they're not ready / I failed."** No method beats the others for every child. Weeks to months is normal, and setbacks are part of learning.
- **"Never start until they ask."** Many children never show obvious signs. This is especially true of children with additional needs. UK continence experts (ERIC) suggest moving out of nappies between about 18 and 30 months for bladder and bowel health, while US sources lean more on readiness cues. Present both: readiness cues help, but you don't have to wait forever.
- **"There's a magic window: too young before 2, too late after 3."** The study often quoted (Hodges 2014) was small, and pointed to **constipation**, not age, as the real problem.
- **"Night pull-ups stop them learning to be dry at night."** Night dryness depends on a hormone and the brain waking to a full bladder. It can't be trained, and most children are dry at night by 5–6.
- **"Cut drinks to keep them dry."** This leads to a smaller bladder, urine infections and constipation. Drink well in the day, and stop about 1 hour before bed.
- **"Lift them to wee at night."** It's a short-term convenience, not training, and can teach weeing while asleep.
- **"Poo holding is stubbornness, so be firm or make them sit."** It's usually fear of pain from constipation. Soft poos plus low pressure fix it. Poo accidents after holding can be overflow, which needs a doctor, not discipline. Even daily poos can still mean constipation.
- **"Don't bother the GP about poo."** Constipation affects up to about 1 in 3 children, and is the first thing to check in any regression.
- **"Punish or shame accidents so they learn."** Every expert body says this slows learning. What works is a calm "wee goes in the potty", with the child helping to clean up as a job.
- **"Rewards always work" or "Rewards ruin it."** Small, immediate rewards help many children (especially just for poo, or just for sitting if they're scared). Big, escalating rewards backfire for anxious kids. Keep them small and fade them out.
- **"Ask every 15 minutes."** The child sits before the bladder is full, nothing comes, and they don't learn their own signal. Prompt at transitions or about every hour, and watch for their tells.
- **"Boys are just lazy."** Boys train a little later on average and hold poo more often. It isn't laziness. Let them sit first and stand later.
- **"Regression means they were never trained."** A new sibling, a move, starting daycare, illness, constipation or a urine infection are common triggers. It usually settles in days to weeks.
- **"Accidents at daycare mean daycare is doing it wrong, or the child is faking."** Busy new settings cause accidents for weeks to months. Fix it with scheduled prompts and easy access, not blame.
