# Nights, naps and bedwetting

## Why nights come later
- Night dryness depends on three things maturing on their own timetable, separate from daytime skills:
  1. **Night hormone (vasopressin/ADH)** that makes the kidneys produce less, more concentrated wee overnight. Low levels: wetting in the first 2–3 hours of sleep, large wet patch, pale wee.
  2. **Bladder capacity and stability.** Small or "overactive" bladder: wets more than once a night, small patches, may wake after wetting.
  3. **Waking to the full-bladder signal.** Not "too deep a sleeper": the brain doesn't yet notice the signal in sleep. Sleeps through wetting, often later in the night.
- It runs strongly in families. Boys are about twice as likely as girls until about 10. Constipation makes it worse (a full bowel presses on the bladder).
- It is never laziness or poor training, and trying harder doesn't speed it up.

## What's normal (quote ranges, not exact numbers)
- Most children become dry at night between 3 and 5. Daytime dryness usually comes months or years earlier.
- About 1 in 7 five-year-olds still wet the bed sometimes, and about 1 in 10 at 6–7. About 5% at 10.
- It resolves on its own for roughly 1 in 10 affected children each year.
- Under 5: normal, no treatment needed. 5+: help is available if it bothers the child or family. Many doctors don't treat before 6–7 unless there are other symptoms.

## When to try nights without a nappy
Any of these:
- dry or nearly dry nappies most mornings for 1–2 weeks;
- reliably dry in the day for 6+ months;
- the child asks to stop night nappies.

How to run the trial:
- Choose a calm 1–2 weeks: no travel, new baby, house move or illness.
- Put protection on the bed, not the child, so they can feel wet.
- Judge after 1–2 weeks, not one night.
- **Mostly dry or improving:** keep going.
- **Soaked almost every night with no change:** go back to night pants without fuss and retry in 1–3 months. Say "not yet", never "you failed".
- **5+ and still wet:** a nappy-free trial of 1–2 weeks is still worthwhile, because feeling wet can be the trigger. If it's still wet after that, see the GP.

## Naps
- Naps are usually dry before nights, so they're good practice.
- Offer a potty sit right before the nap. Keep big drinks to earlier, and put a waterproof layer under the child.
- If naps are often dry, drop the nap nappy.

## Fluids
- **Don't cut drinks across the day.** It doesn't help and can backfire. A thirsty child drinks more at night, and concentrated wee irritates the bladder.
- Offer drinks evenly: one at each meal and snack, mostly water (milk is fine). Ages 4–8 need roughly 1–1.4 litres a day. Toddlers: regular drinks through the day.
- Front-load the day. Most drinks should be before late afternoon.
- **Last drink about 1 hour before bed**, then nothing until morning (a small sip is fine if they're really thirsty). Some sources say 2 hours; 1 hour is enough for a well-hydrated child.
- No caffeine: cola, iced tea, hot chocolate, energy drinks. Avoid fizzy and sugary drinks after lunch.
- Check poos: fewer than 3 a week, hard or large poos, straining or soiling means treat constipation first. Night wetting often improves once poos are soft.

## Bedtime routine
1. A normal drink with dinner, then stop about 1 hour before bed.
2. Wind down with a book and low lights, and no screens in the last hour.
3. **Bedtime wee** as part of getting ready: relaxed, no rushing. Do a "last wee" right before lights out if more than 30 minutes have passed.
4. Make night trips easy: a night light, a potty in the bedroom, pyjamas that are easy to pull down.
5. Tell them: "If you feel a wee coming in the night, the potty is right there, and you can call me any time."
6. In the morning, wee straight after waking, and praise the routine, not the result.

## Lifting or dream-wees (carrying a sleeping child to the toilet)
- NHS, NICE and children's hospitals: don't rely on it. It may save a sheet, but it doesn't teach dryness, and can teach weeing while asleep.
- It's okay as a short-term fix in a tough week (a holiday, a new baby). Be honest that it isn't training.
- Do help if the child wakes up on their own.

## Bed setup: "double-make" the bed
- From the bottom up: waterproof mattress protector, absorbent pad or bed mat, sheet, then a second pad or mat and a second sheet on top.
- After a wet, strip only the top layer. No remaking the bed at 2am.
- For very wet children, add waterproof duvet and pillow protectors.

## Handling a wet night
1. Low light, quiet voice, brief and matter-of-fact. "Uh-oh, a wee came out. Let's get you dry and cosy."
2. Wee in the toilet, change pyjamas, strip the top layer, back to bed.
3. In the morning they can help put pyjamas in the wash basket. That's responsibility, never punishment.
4. Don't discuss it at breakfast or in front of siblings, and allow no teasing.

## Rewards at night
- **Reward what the child can control, not dry nights:**
  - drinking their water during the day;
  - doing the bedtime wee;
  - getting up to the potty;
  - helping change the bed.
- Never take away stickers they've already earned. Rewarding dry nights rewards something their body can't do yet, and adds shame.

## Bedwetting alarms (age 5+, usually most successful from 6–7)
- A moisture sensor sounds or vibrates at the first drops. Over weeks, the brain learns "full bladder means wake up".
- They help about half to three-quarters of children, and are the option most likely to give lasting dryness. Don't promise a result.
- **How to use one:**
  - Use it every night. The child helps set it up and switches it on.
  - Put the unit far enough away that they must get out of bed to turn it off.
  - **Expect to help at first.** Many children don't wake to it in the early weeks, so a parent wakes them fully and walks them through it: turn it off, finish on the toilet, change, reset.
  - Signs it's working: smaller patches, waking faster, wetting later in the night.
  - Review at about 4 weeks. Continue until 14 dry nights in a row, which usually takes 6–8 weeks.
  - If it's not improving after 4 weeks, talk to the GP. If wetting returns, using the alarm again often works.
- Make the child the hero: "This alarm is like a coach for your brain."

## Medicines
- Desmopressin is a medicine some doctors prescribe from about 6. It reduces night-time wee on the nights it's taken (useful for sleepovers and camps). It isn't a cure.
- With desmopressin, fluids must be limited from 1 hour before to 8 hours after the dose, and it's paused during vomiting or diarrhoea.
- **The coach never suggests doses or starting medicine. Always say: talk to your GP.**

## See a doctor
**Book a GP visit (not urgent):**
- 5+ and still wetting despite a good routine, or it's upsetting the child;
- wetting starts again after 6+ months dry (also think about stress: a new sibling, school, bullying);
- daytime symptoms: urgency, daytime wetting, weeing more than 7 or fewer than 4 times a day, straining or a weak stream;
- constipation or poo accidents;
- loud snoring or pauses in breathing at night;
- repeated urine infections;
- no improvement after 4 weeks with an alarm.

**See a doctor the same day:**
- pain or burning when weeing, fever, or the child seems unwell (possible urine infection);
- blood or pink or cloudy wee;
- very thirsty, weeing large amounts, tired, losing weight (possible diabetes).

## Words that help
- To the child:
  - "Lots of kids' bodies are still learning to stay dry at night. It's not your fault, and you're not in trouble."
  - "Your brain and bladder are still learning to talk to each other while you sleep."
- To the parent:
  - "Two weeks is a fair test. Going back to night pants isn't failure. It just means not yet."
  - "Night dryness depends on a hormone that matures on its own timetable."

## Never
- Punish, shame or call it lazy. Remove earned rewards. Let siblings tease. Compare with other children.
- Restrict drinks all day. Threaten nappies. Buy "cure" products with big claims.
